## Problem and purpose

### Why start with the problem

A business case answers one question for a sponsor: why should we spend money and attention on this? A credential programme is a means. The problem it solves is the reason. Many proposals start with the means ("we want to issue digital badges") and then look for a problem. Sponsors notice this and ask for the problem first.

A good problem statement has four parts:

1. **Who** has the problem. Name the group and, where you can, the size of the group.
2. **What** the problem is, in a sentence a sponsor can repeat.
3. **Evidence** that the problem exists. This can be data, interviews, complaints, audit findings or a gap in the market.
4. **Consequence** of doing nothing. This is what happens if the sponsor says no.

A credential is evidence that a person has met a defined standard. It can help when the problem is that skills, learning or achievements are hard to see, compare or trust. It does not help when the real problem is something else, such as poor learning design or a lack of jobs. Say so honestly in your case. Sponsors trust a case that names its limits.

### Purpose in one sentence

The purpose of the programme is the change you want to see. Write it as one sentence: "For [group], the programme makes [thing] visible and trusted so that [change]."

Example pattern: "For warehouse supervisors, the programme makes leadership skills visible and trusted so that internal vacancies are filled by people who already work here."

Check the purpose with two tests. First, can a sponsor picture the change? Second, can you measure it later? If not, rewrite it.

### Skills-based hiring and internal mobility

Skills-based hiring and internal mobility are widely discussed in the field, and practice varies from one organisation to another. Credentials can act as the evidence layer in these approaches: they give a hiring manager or a talent team a record of what someone has shown, who assessed it and against which standard. Status of this topic: Established. Do not claim more than this in your case. Describe your own context and your own evidence instead of quoting general claims.

### Worked example and practice

**Worked example: Problem statement in a corporate academy**

Context: the corporate academy of a mid-sized logistics company. All numbers are illustrative.

- Who: 400 warehouse supervisors and the regional operations managers who fill supervisor and team-manager roles.
- What: leadership skills are learned on the job and in short courses, but nobody can see them across sites. Vacancies for team managers are often filled from outside.
- Evidence: in the last year, 14 of 20 team-manager vacancies were filled externally (illustrative). Interviews with three operations managers show that they do not trust attendance certificates.
- Consequence: recruitment costs stay high and strong supervisors leave for employers that recognise their development.
- Purpose: "For warehouse supervisors, the programme makes leadership skills visible and trusted so that more team-manager vacancies are filled by people who already work here."

Notice what the example leaves out: it does not mention badges, platforms or technology. That comes later.

**Worked example: Problem statement in a training provider**

Context: a vocational college that runs a short course in digital marketing. All numbers are illustrative.

- Who: about 250 adult learners per year, many changing careers, and the small employers who hire them.
- What: learners finish the course, but employers cannot tell what a graduate can actually do, so graduates must prove themselves again at every interview.
- Evidence: employer advisory board feedback and a follow-up survey in which 9 of 30 graduates said that employers did not understand their certificate (illustrative).
- Consequence: the course loses reputation against competitors that publish clear learning outcomes.
- Purpose: "For adult career changers, the programme makes tested digital marketing skills visible and trusted so that more graduates are shortlisted for entry-level roles."

**Practice activity: Write your problem statement**

1. Choose your programme idea.
2. Write the four parts: who, what, evidence, consequence. Use two to four sentences each.
3. Mark any part where you have no evidence yet. Write down how you will get it.
4. Write the purpose sentence using the pattern in this unit.
5. Test it: could a sponsor picture the change, and could you measure it later?

Keep your text. It becomes the first section of your business case.

[( )] The academy should buy a credential platform this year.
[( )] Learners like badges and would share them on social media.
****

****

**Question 2.** What is the main reason to state the consequence of doing nothing?

[( )] It makes the document longer and more formal.
[( )] It shows that the programme will be cheap.
[(X)] It shows the sponsor what the decision to say no would cost.
[( )] It replaces the need for evidence.
****
A clear consequence gives the sponsor a reason to act. It does not replace evidence and it says nothing about cost.
****

**Question 3.** A manager says: "Our real problem is that our courses are poor." What is the most honest response in a business case?

[( )] Propose a credential programme anyway, because credentials fix poor courses.
[( )] Remove the problem statement and focus on technology.
[(X)] Say that a credential will not fix course quality, and propose to address that first or alongside.
[( )] Argue that credentials make the problem invisible.
****
A credential makes achievement visible and trusted. It does not improve learning design. Naming the limit builds trust with the sponsor.

**Question 4.** Which sentence is the best purpose statement?

[( )] The programme will be launched in the spring.
[( )] The programme will use a modern, flexible platform.
[(X)] For adult career changers, the programme makes tested skills visible and trusted so that more graduates are shortlisted for entry-level roles.
[( )] The programme will make everyone happier.
****
A good purpose describes a change for a named group that can be measured later. The other options describe a date, a tool or a mood.

## Value for three parties

### Three parties, three value propositions

A credential programme only works when it is worth something to the people who take part. In most programmes three parties matter:

- **Earners**: the people who complete the learning or assessment and receive the credential.
- **Issuer**: the organisation that designs, assesses and awards the credential.
- **Verifiers**: the people or organisations that check and use the credential, such as employers, admissions teams, clients or regulators.

Write one value proposition for each party. A value proposition says what the party gets, in terms the party would use. If you cannot write it in their words, you have not yet talked to them.

### Value for earners

Earners want recognition, a clearer path and some control over their own record. Typical value for earners includes:

- a record of skills they can show to others;
- a clear standard to work towards;
- the ability to carry the record across jobs and learning providers;
- in some contexts, access to a role, a promotion or a further course.

Ask what the earner must do and give up. Time, effort and sometimes fees are costs for earners. A credential that costs more effort than it returns will not be taken up.

### Value for the issuer

The issuer is often the sponsor's own organisation. Typical value for issuers includes:

- clearer learning outcomes and assessment standards;
- a trusted record that reflects the organisation's reputation;
- better data on who has learned what;
- stronger relationships with employers, members or learners;
- in some cases, income from fees.

Issuers also carry duties. They must keep the standard, the assessment and the records in good order for as long as the credential is in use. Include this in the case.

### Value for verifiers

Verifiers want to make better decisions faster and with less risk. Typical value for verifiers includes:

- evidence that is easier to check than a paper certificate;
- a clear description of what the credential means and who assessed it;
- less time spent on enquiries and screening;
- more confidence in candidates whose skills they cannot observe directly.

Verifiers will only use a credential they understand and trust. That depends on the quality of the standard and the assessment, which is why quality belongs in the value discussion. In ICoBC terms, this is the Value pillar of VRITE: value that all three parties can see.

### Worked example and practice

**Worked example: Three value propositions in a human resources department**

Context: the HR department of a regional health services provider, which wants to credential its internal mentoring programme for nurses. Numbers are illustrative.

- Earner (a nurse who completes the programme): "I get a record of my mentoring skills that I can use in promotion discussions and that stays with me if I move to another employer."
- Issuer (the HR department and the nursing directorate): "We get a shared standard for mentoring, data on how many mentors we have trained at each site, and a stronger case for our development budget."
- Verifier (a ward manager choosing a mentor for new staff): "I can see quickly who has completed the programme, what the standard was and who assessed it, without phoning the academy."

Each proposition uses the language of the party. The ward manager does not care about platforms. They care about finding a mentor they can trust.

**Worked example: Three value propositions in a university**

Context: a university that wants to credential a short course in research data skills for doctoral candidates. Numbers are illustrative.

- Earner (a doctoral candidate): "I get an assessed record of my data skills that I can attach to funding applications and job applications."
- Issuer (the graduate school): "We get a consistent standard across faculties and a way to show funders that our candidates receive training."
- Verifier (a research team leader): "I can see which applicants have shown data handling skills before I read their full application."

**Practice activity: Three value propositions**

1. Take your programme idea and your problem statement.
2. Write one value proposition each for the earner, the issuer and the verifier. Use their own words and one or two sentences each.
3. For each party, add one sentence: what does this party give up (time, money, effort, risk)?
4. Choose one party you have not spoken to. Plan one short conversation or message to test your sentence.
5. Add the three propositions to your draft case.

<summary>Facilitator notes</summary>

Workshop time: about 60 minutes. Use role play in groups of three: one earner, one issuer, one verifier. Each person reads their value proposition aloud and the others say whether they would act on it. Ask groups to swap and hear a proposition from another programme. Note which party is hardest to write for; this is usually the verifier, because verifiers are often outside the sponsor's organisation.

## Costs

### What a credential programme costs

Costs are easier to estimate than benefits, so sponsors often start there. A realistic cost estimate builds trust. A cost estimate that is too low will come back as a problem later. Estimate costs for the three parties, not only for the issuer, and show the costs over time.

### Cost categories for the issuer

Use a simple list so nothing is forgotten:

- **Design**: defining the standard, learning outcomes, assessment methods and criteria, and the credential description.
- **Delivery and assessment**: staff time for teaching, assessing, moderating and quality assurance.
- **Technology**: systems to issue, store and share credentials, integration with existing systems, and security and privacy review.
- **Operations**: support for earners and verifiers, administration, record keeping, revocation and re-issue processes.
- **Communication**: telling earners and verifiers what the credential means and how to check it.
- **Governance and quality**: reviewing the standard, handling appeals, keeping the programme current.
- **Legal and privacy**: advice on data protection and contract terms. <!-- VERIFY: Check the data protection rules that apply in the jurisdiction of the issuer before finalising this item. -->

### Costs for earners and verifiers

Earners may pay in fees, time off work, travel and effort. Even when the issuer pays the fees, time is a cost, and sponsors should see it. In a corporate academy, the time of a supervisor is paid time that is not spent on operations.

Verifiers pay in time to learn what the credential means and to build it into their routines. If a hiring team must change a screening process, that is a cost. You do not need exact figures for verifiers. You need to show that you have considered them and have a plan to keep the burden small.

### One-off and running costs

Separate costs that happen once from costs that continue:

- **One-off (set-up)**: design, system selection and set-up, pilot, initial communication.
- **Running (per year)**: delivery, assessment, support, licences and hosting, quality review.

A programme that looks cheap in year one can be expensive in year three if running costs were ignored. Show at least three years. Use ranges when you are unsure, and state your assumptions.

## Benefits and evidence

### From value to measurable benefits

In Module 2 you described value in words. A sponsor will also want benefits they can check. A benefit is a change that matters to someone and that you can observe. Link each benefit to a party, to a measure and to a source of evidence.

Types of benefit:

- **Financial**: costs avoided or income gained, such as lower external recruitment cost or fee income.
- **Operational**: time saved, faster decisions, fewer enquiries.
- **Strategic**: stronger reputation, more employer partnerships, better alignment with a policy goal.
- **Human**: progress, confidence, recognition for earners. These are real, and you can measure some of them by survey or by follow-up.

Do not turn every benefit into money. Forcing a figure on a benefit that cannot be counted weakens the case. Say what is financial, what is measurable but not financial, and what is a judgement.

### Outputs, outcomes and success measures

Distinguish three levels:

- **Outputs**: what the programme produces, such as the number of credentials issued.
- **Outcomes**: what changes for the parties, such as the share of vacancies filled internally.
- **Impact**: the longer-term result, such as lower turnover or better career progression.

Outputs are easy to count and weak on their own. A programme can issue thousands of credentials and change nothing. Choose a small set of success measures, mostly outcomes, and add one or two outputs to show progress. Three to six measures are enough.

For each measure, state:

1. the definition and how it is measured;
2. the baseline (where you are now);
3. the target and the date;
4. who collects it and where it is recorded.

### Evidence and decision points

Evidence is how you will know. Sources can include system data, surveys, interviews, observation and the credentials themselves. Say how much effort the evidence will take to collect. A measure that nobody has time to collect will be dropped.

Decision points are moments at which the sponsor decides whether to continue, change or stop. They turn a one-off approval into a managed series of choices. Typical decision points are:

- **Go or no-go** after the pilot, with agreed criteria.
- **Scale or adjust** at the end of year one.
- **Continue or retire** at a yearly review.

Write the criteria in advance: "If fewer than 60 of 120 planned earners complete by month nine, we review the design before scaling." Sponsors like to know that they can stop.

## The case document

### Structure of the case

A business case is a short document that helps a person decide. It should be readable in ten to fifteen minutes. Use a clear structure so the sponsor can find what they need:

1. **Summary**: the problem, the proposal, the cost and the ask, on one page.
2. **Problem and purpose**: who, what, evidence, consequence, and the purpose sentence.
3. **Value for three parties**: one proposition each for earners, issuer and verifiers.
4. **Costs**: one-off and running, by year, with assumptions.
5. **Benefits and success measures**: measures with baselines, targets and evidence sources.
6. **Risks and limits**: what could go wrong and what the programme does not do.
7. **Options**: at least one alternative, including doing nothing.
8. **Plan and decision points**: pilot, review dates and criteria.
9. **The ask**: exactly what you want the sponsor to decide.

Write the summary last. Put the ask in the first paragraph and repeat it at the end.

### Risks, options and the ask

A sponsor will ask "what could go wrong?" Answer before they do. List three to five risks, such as low take-up, verifiers who do not use the credential, cost overrun and loss of key staff. For each, state the likelihood, the effect and what you will do. Keep it short and honest.

Options show that you considered alternatives. Describe at least two: doing nothing, a smaller version (for example a pilot in one site), and your preferred option. Say why you prefer the one you do. If a different option is cheaper but does not solve the problem, say so.

The ask is a specific decision: "Approve a pilot for 30 supervisors in two regions with a budget of up to X, and a review in month 9." A vague ask gets a vague answer.

### The five-minute pitch

Many sponsors decide after a short conversation, not after reading. A five-minute pitch needs a simple structure:

- **Minute 1**: the problem and who has it. One concrete example.
- **Minute 2**: the proposal and the value for the three parties.
- **Minute 3**: costs and benefits in plain figures, with the main assumption.
- **Minute 4**: risks, decision points and what you will measure.
- **Minute 5**: the ask, repeated, and a question to the sponsor.

Use no more than five slides, or none. Slides with one idea each work better than slides full of text. Prepare answers to three likely questions: "What does it cost?", "How will we know it worked?" and "What if it fails?". Practise aloud with a timer.

Write as you would speak to a busy colleague: plain words, short sentences, no jargon the sponsor has not heard. Say "we do not yet know" when it is true.
